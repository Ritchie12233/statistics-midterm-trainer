What Are Data?
• Data: Descriptions of the world around us.
• Collected through careful observation (e.g., field notes, surveys, and 
experiments)
• Stored on computers to allow us to infer properties about the world
• Data form the backbone of any statistical investigation



--- PAGE 8 ---
Defining 
Data 
Science
Data Science: The discipline of drawing 
useful conclusions from large and 
diverse data sets using computation
It combines statistical ideas with 
fundamental computer science 
concepts
Focuses on a minimal set of core 
techniques applied to real-world 
applications

The Three Core Pillars of Data Science



--- PAGE 10 ---
Pillar 1 — Exploration
• Definition: Identifying patterns in information
• Goal: To summarize data concisely, look for differences, and 
check for inconsistencies
• Primary Tools:
• Visualizations (graphs, charts)
• Descriptive statistics


--- PAGE 11 ---



--- PAGE 12 ---
Pillar 2 — Prediction
• Definition: Using information we know to make informed guesses 
about values we wish we knew
• Goal: To accurately forecast unknown or future outcomes based 
on historical data
• Primary Tools:
• Machine learning
• Optimization algorithms


--- PAGE 13 ---



--- PAGE 14 ---
Pillar 3 — Inference
• Definition: Quantifying our degree of certainty
• Goal: To determine if patterns found in our sample data will also 
appear in new observations
• Primary Tools:
• Statistical tests
• Statistical models

Why Do We Need Data Science?
Decision Making under Uncertainty: Most important decisions are 
made with only partial information
Reducing Uncertainty: Access to large datasets and computational 
tools sharply reduces the degree of uncertainty

finance, medicine, advertising, manufacturing, and social sciences

Where Does Statistics Fit In?
• The Core Challenge: Drawing robust conclusions about the world 
using incomplete information
• The Solution: Statistics studies exactly how to do this
• Statistics is a central, foundational component of the entire data 
science workflow

Resampling & 
Randomization over 
Pure Math
• The Problem with Traditional Math: 
Real data rarely follow regular 
patterns or perfectly match standard 
equations
• The Computational Solution:
• Using randomness to imagine 
many possible scenarios 
consistent with the data
• Resampling techniques require 
fewer assumptions and conditions 
than advanced traditional math



--- PAGE 21 ---
The Danger of Anecdotal Evidence
• Anecdotal Evidence: Data collected in a haphazard 
fashion, often based on unpublished, personal 
stories
• Example: "I met two students who took 7 years to 
graduate, so it must take longer to graduate here 
than at other colleges"
• The Issue: May be true, but only represents 
extraordinary or unusual cases



--- PAGE 22 ---
Why Anecdotal Evidence Fails
• Anecdotal evidence usually fails standard statistical evaluations 
because of:
• Small Sample Size: Too few observations to see natural variation
• Selection Bias: People interested in a topic are more likely to share 
unusual data
• Confirmation Bias: People cite examples that confirm their pre-existing 
beliefs
• Inaccuracy: Personal stories are easily misremembered or 
misrepresented

--- PAGE 24 ---
The Statistical Solution: Generalizing to a 
Population
• The Alternative to Anecdotes: Rigorous data collection and 
inference
• Population: The entire group or set of possible cases
• Sample: A subset of the population from which data are collected
• Inference: Using data from a randomly selected sample to 
estimate characteristics of the general population

Cases, Variables, and Constants
Case (Observational Unit): The 
individual unit from which data are 
collected
Examples: A person, an animal (subject), a 
county, or a loan
Variable: A characteristic of a case that is measured and can take on 
different values (i.e., it varies)
Constant: A characteristic that is the exact same for all cases in a 
research study


--- PAGE 27 ---
Organizing Data — The Data Matrix
• Data Matrix: A convenient and common way to organize tabular 
data, especially in spreadsheets or programming
• Rows = Cases: Each row corresponds to a single, unique case
• Columns = Variables: Each column represents a specific 
characteristic measured for those cases
• In Python: This structure is called a DataFrame, provided by the 
Pandas library


--- PAGE 28 ---
Broad Types of Variables
• Variables must be classified into types to determine which 
statistical tools and graphs are appropriate
• The two main branches of variables are:
• Categorical (Qualitative): Represent groupings, names, or labels
• Quantitative (Numerical): Represent numerical values with meaningful 
magnitudes


--- PAGE 29 ---
Categorical 
Variables
Categorical Variables: Provide groupings that 
may have no logical order or a logical order with 
inconsistent differences between groups
Also known as qualitative variables
Examples:
• Continent of birth
• Preferred ice cream flavor (chocolate, vanilla, strawberry)


--- PAGE 30 ---
Nominal vs. Ordinal Categorical Variables
Nominal (Unordered): 
Categories with no natural 
or logical ordering
Example: US State of 
residence (AL, AK, 
WY)
Ordinal (Ordered): 
Categories that have a 
logical, natural ordering, 
but the "distance" between 
categories is inconsistent
Example: Highest 
level of education 
(High school, 2-year 
degree, 4-year 
degree, PhD)


--- PAGE 31 ---
Quantitative 
Variables
Quantitative Variables: Numerical values 
with meaningful magnitudes that can be 
placed in order with consistent intervals
Also known as numerical variables
Math operations (adding, subtracting, 
averaging) make logical sense
Examples: Weight in kilograms, distance 
run in miles, or highway mile markers


--- PAGE 32 ---
Continuous vs. Discrete Quantitative Variables
Continuous: Can take on any wide 
range of numerical values, 
including infinite decimal places
Example: A student's height or the 
unemployment rate
Discrete: Can only take numerical 
values with jumps (usually non-
negative whole numbers)
Example: The number of siblings a student 
has, or the population count of a county


--- PAGE 33 ---
Relationships: 
Associated vs. 
Independent
• Associated (Dependent) Variables: Two 
variables that show a discernible pattern or 
connection with one another
• Positive Association: As one goes up, the 
other goes up (e.g., median household 
income and population change)
• Negative Association: As one goes up, the 
other goes down (e.g., multi-unit structures 
and homeownership rates)
• Independent Variables: Two variables that show 
absolutely no evident relationship


--- PAGE 34 ---
Real-World Data Structures & Missing Data
• Raw Data: Data that has not been processed after collection
• Data Validation: The process of computing basic statistics and 
comparing them to known results to check for import errors
• Handling Missing Data:
• Real-world data contains unknown or inapplicable values
• These often appear as NaN ("Not a Number") in Python
• Data Cleaning: The process of identifying errors, replacing special 
error codes (like 97, 98, 99) with NaN, and computing new 
variables
